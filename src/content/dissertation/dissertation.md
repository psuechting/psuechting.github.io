Who bears the burden of fossil-fuel electricity generation, and who receives the benefits of what is replacing it? This dissertation poses that distributive question at the generating facility and resolves it into three: who installs emissions control equipment; who operates it, and how cleanly; and who hosts the capacity a decarbonizing fleet builds and retires. Three analyses answer them with federal data: generator censuses and fuel records from Energy Information Administration (EIA) Forms 860 and 923; emissions from the Environmental Protection Agency's Emissions & Generation Resource Integrated Database (eGRID) and Clean Air Markets Program (CAMD) monitors; and community demographics from the American Community Survey (ACS). The first two analyses observe coal- and gas-fired plants of at least 25 megawatts at three snapshots (2009, 2018, and 2021) spanning three regulatory regimes, 4,818 plant-years in all; the third follows the full fleet, renewables included, annually from 2010 through 2023, 13,063 plants and 112,782 plant-years.

Both traditions the design draws on, organizational political economy and environmental justice, predicted disparities in who installs control. Neither prediction holds; both reappear downstream, in how plants run once equipped. Rate-regulated utilities and cooperatives emit more nitrogen oxides (NO<sub>x</sub>) per unit of electricity than merchant generators exposed to market discipline, a penalty that survives accounting for the equipment carried. By 2021, plants in more nonwhite communities likewise run measurably dirtier, independent of income and poverty. The burden is thus concentrating on the more nonwhite communities beside the aging urban plants that rising demand keeps in service, while the buildout's benefits land in whiter states, as solar disperses between states rather than within them. Coal's retirements, against expectation, have whitened its surviving fleet: the plants that closed sat in more nonwhite communities than those that remain.

Organizational structure and community demographics leave their mark downstream of the decision to install, in how equipment already installed is run, and fleet turnover is what redistributes the burden. Encouraging the transition therefore requires regulation that encourages the entry of renewables, discourages the entry of new fossil capacity, and encourages the retirement of old.
